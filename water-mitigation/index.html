Mitigation is the first 24 to 72 hours of a water loss. What gets done in that window decides how much drywall, flooring and insulation has to be torn out later.

Water mitigation is the work of stopping a water loss from spreading and drying the structure to a safe moisture level, using extraction, air movers, dehumidifiers and moisture readings that prove the materials are dry.

When water in your home is an emergency

Treat it as an emergency when water is still running, when it is dripping through a ceiling or light fixture, when it has soaked carpet or pad, or when it has been sitting longer than a day. Active water keeps finding new material to soak, and wet drywall and insulation start to hold moisture that a surface towel will never reach.

Water near outlets, a panel or ceiling fixtures adds an electrical risk. Stay out of that area, cut power only if you can reach the panel dry, and call the utility or 911 for any sparking, burning smell or gas odor before you call us.

My wall feels damp and the baseboard is swelling. Is that water mitigation?

Yes, and it is the stage most people miss. A wall that feels cool and damp, paint that bubbles, baseboards that swell or lift, a musty smell in a closed room, or a floor that suddenly feels soft are all signs that water got past the surface.

The trade name for the fix is water mitigation. It means finding where the water went with moisture meters and a thermal camera, then drying the wall cavity and subfloor in place when that is possible, or opening the wall when it is not.

What a mitigation crew does, in order

  1. Stop or confirm the source. A plumber or the homeowner closes the valve or repairs the line. Drying cannot finish while water is still entering.
  2. Extract standing water. Submersible pumps, wet vacuums and extraction units pull loose water out of carpet, pad and hard floors.
  3. Remove what cannot be saved. Soaked pad, saturated insulation and swollen baseboard come out so the structure can dry. Salvageable materials stay.
  4. Map the moisture. Meters and thermal imaging show where water traveled, including behind walls and under cabinets, and give a starting reading for every material.
  5. Dry with controlled airflow. Air movers push air across wet surfaces and LGR dehumidifiers pull the moisture out of the room.
  6. Check readings daily. Crews compare each wet material against a dry reference reading from an unaffected part of the home and stop only when the numbers match.

Structural drying: why the equipment stays for days

Structural drying is the part of mitigation that happens after the water is gone from view. Wet drywall, framing, subfloor and plaster release moisture slowly, so the room is run as a closed drying chamber with air movers aimed at the walls and dehumidifiers pulling the humidity out of the air.

Most homes need several days of equipment time, and dense materials such as hardwood, plaster and thick framing take longer than carpet or drywall. The equipment is checked each day, moved if a reading stalls, and removed only when every material reaches its dry target under the ANSI/IICRC S500 water damage standard.

Where water hides in Poway homes

Many Poway homes sit on concrete slabs, and a slab does not tell you where water went. A leak under a kitchen or bath floor can travel sideways along the slab edge and show up as a damp baseboard in the next room, so crews map moisture across several rooms before they place equipment.

After a storm, a common pattern is water that came in at a window or a door threshold and wicked into the wall below it. Those leaks look small from the inside, and they are the reason crews carry a thermal camera as well as a meter.

How insurance usually treats mitigation

Sudden and accidental water damage, such as a burst pipe or an appliance failure, is often covered by a homeowners policy, while gradual leaks and long-term neglect often are not. Flood damage from rising water is usually excluded and needs separate flood coverage. Check your policy or ask your insurer, because wording differs between carriers.

Whatever your coverage, photograph the damage before anything is moved, keep receipts, and ask the crew for the moisture readings and scope notes. Those documents are what an adjuster asks for.
